    How the Shred Hole works

    Silly premise, real physics underneath.

    • The fan is a 420 kg·m² rotor driven by a 1.2 MW speed-controlled motor, fighting an air load that grows with ω² (the fan law). Tunnel airspeed follows blade speed. Drag the slider below the centre line and the rotor runs backwards: the pitched blades move air up the tube at about 65% efficiency, which makes the tube a vertical wind tunnel. Light things float or get blown out of the top, and heavy things sink against the updraft into the blades.
    • Blade strikes are momentum exchanges: the blade surface (pitched 22–52°) hits the object with the reduced mass of object + rotor and 25% restitution. Heavy things visibly slow the fan.
    • Breakability is worked out from materials, size and mass; nothing is hand-tuned. Mass ÷ volume against the material's density says how solid a thing is: a fridge works out to ~1 mm walls, a boulder is solid. From that come the energy to break it (work of fracture × fracture area) and the force needed to cut into it (crushing strength × the solid part of the contact). A blade bends at 250 kN and snaps at 600 kN. If something is stronger than the blade, the blade loses: it bends, snaps, or the rotor stalls against it. Then the breaker trips and the fan is out of order until you repair it.
    • The air has a turbulent boundary layer growing from the intake lip, a sink-like intake that sucks things in, flow splitting around the hub, swirl behind the rotor and a spreading exit jet. Free-stream turbulence is a sum of divergence-free random Fourier modes, advected with the flow.
    • Aerodynamics are computed per surface facet. Each facet gets impact pressure on the windward side, wake suction on the leeward side and skin friction, which is what makes things tumble. Thin flat things use a flat-plate model with a moving centre of pressure (Andersen, Pesavento & Wang 2005), so cards and paper planes flutter. Spinning balls get Magnus lift and everything gets buoyancy (helium balloons rise).
    • Breaking splits objects into their parts, or shatters them into convex chunks that are real rigid bodies. Fragile things also break on hard landings.
    • The portal pulls debris into a swirling vortex below the tower. Each piece's shape (a convex hull), colour, mass and velocity are packed into a debris drop and sent to World Builder, where they fall out of the sky onto a growing pile.
    • Sound is synthesised. The hum is the blade-pass frequency (blades × rev/s), and wind noise follows the airspeed.

    Rigid bodies by Rapier, rendering by three.js. A 2D CFD version (lattice-Boltzmann) lives in the Lab, the original wind tunnel. Every Shred Hole object is there as a side-view silhouette at real size (and anything from the Warehouse): pick one from an object's card (🔬) to see real flow round it.
